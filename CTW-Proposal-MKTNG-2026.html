Executive Summary

CTW is a different firm than the one its current website describes.

The firm has moved toward advisory-led work with middle-market owners and CFOs through capital events. The website still talks like a generalist compliance shop with twenty-eight industries and sixty service lines. That gap is the opportunity.

We propose to close it in four weeks for the core build. Twelve weeks for the full launch with original photography. Three engagement modes let CTW choose how deep the partnership goes. Path Forward Foundation rebuilds the site itself. Path Forward Engine adds a podcast, original research, and a content production engine. Path Forward Partnership runs the full ongoing program with quarterly business reviews and AEO citation tracking.

Three things make this proposal different from the others CTW will receive.

First, the technical foundation is built for the next decade, not the last one. WordPress has been the backbone of half the internet for two decades, and it's served the web well. The platform isn't what it was. It's gotten heavy and fragile, and most modern WordPress sites are held together by a stack of plugins that quietly fight each other. Most agencies will recommend another WordPress build because it's what they know. We're recommending a different path. CTW's new site will load in under a second on any device, cost a fraction to host, hold up against the security threats hitting professional services firms over the last three years, and is built for how prospects will be finding accounting firms five years from now. The technology underneath is Astro on Cloudflare with Keystatic as the editor. The benefit is a site that's faster, safer, cheaper to run, and easier to keep current.

Second, the architecture is built for AI discovery, not just for Google. Schema-rich, FAQ-structured, plain-language content that gets cited by ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google AI Overviews when prospects ask "who's the right CPA firm for a middle-market construction company in Northern California." This is where the next decade of professional services discovery is happening. We intend to win it. (We've already run a baseline AEO analysis for CTW; the headline finding sits in Section 6.)

Third, the work doesn't end at launch. Building a great website is the easy part. Sustaining it with content that compounds, in a partner's actual voice, across years, is the part most firms skip and most agencies don't sell. Anyone can ship a website. Few can sustain the human work that makes one matter. Our experience is in that side. Writing in a partner's voice. Producing a podcast that turns guest interviews into relationships with the people who matter to the firm. Running press release programs that compound. Training internal teams to use AI without losing the firm's character.

We've spent more than a decade building communications programs for firms whose work is watched closely. Construction and infrastructure clients whose projects land in real communities. Energy and utility programs that face public hearings and regulatory scrutiny. Real estate engagements where a single transaction is worth more than most firms' annual revenue. Public-sector contracts where every dollar gets audited. Accounting work has the same character. The audience pays attention. The work has to earn its place. The voice has to hold up. We know how to write for that, and we know how to keep doing it past launch week. We aren't the cheapest bidder. We don't intend to be the most expensive either. We are the firm CTW will still be working with in 2030.

Reading the Moment

CTW's recent work tells a clear story. Capital events for middle-market owners. Quality of earnings studies for buyers. Tax structuring for sellers. FAR compliance for government contractors. Multi-state audits for manufacturers and distributors. The firm is good at high-stakes, multi-year, advisory-led engagements.

You already know the current website doesn't reflect any of that. A few things we picked up in the initial review:

  • The home hero rotates between four messages with no anchoring position
  • The services architecture lists roughly sixty offerings across four pillars
  • The industries page names twenty-eight verticals, which is the opposite of specialization
  • The newsroom's most recent post is from 2020

This is lag, not failure. Most professional services websites run two or three years behind the firms they represent. Section 2

Approach and Methodology

The four-week core commitment is real because the work behind it is staged. Each phase has a defined input, a defined output, and a clean handoff to what follows. The longer engagement modes extend this same process into the year after launch, with the same senior team running the work throughout.

Phase 0: Brand and Messaging Sprint (Week 1)

A focused working session with CTW's leadership locks the firm's position, the value proposition, and the audience-specific framings for the four prospect types: business owners, CFOs, PE sponsors, and capital markets prospects. The output is a brand voice document delivered as both a printed reference guide and a structured AI skill file CTW keeps permanently. The skill file means CTW can generate on-brand content with AI tools indefinitely, without recreating the voice guidelines each time. Everything written from that point forward runs against this document.

Phase 1: Architecture, Content Audit, and AEO Foundation (Week 2)

The existing site gets audited page by page, with a documented retain, rewrite, or retire recommendation against each one. The AEO baseline expands from the eight queries we ran during proposal preparation to twenty-five priority queries mapped to CTW's actual practice areas and named geographies. Sitemap gets finalized jointly with CTW's leadership. Schema architecture is defined. The technical foundation on Astro, Cloudflare, and Keystatic initializes in parallel with the audit so no time is lost.

Phase 2: Design and Content Production (Week 3)

Joshua presents three design directions to CTW's leadership for selection. The chosen direction becomes the component library and visual system for the full site. Content rewrite begins on the priority pages identified in the audit. Where CTW writes, we edit and structure for AEO. Where we write, the copy runs through the brand voice document so the firm's tone holds throughout. CTW reviews all content before it goes live.

Phase 3: Build, Integration, and QA (Week 4)

Development runs against the approved design and audited content. Calendar booking integrates with Microsoft 365. The Capital Event Readiness Assessment, the two calculators, and the CPACharge payment flow all connect. Accessibility testing runs against WCAG 2.2 AA. The site goes to soft launch for CTW's leadership review at the end of the week. CTW selects one design direction by end of week three to hold the four-week delivery commitment.

Phase 4: Launch, Training, and Stabilization (Week 5 and Beyond)

Public launch with 301 redirects mapped from old URLs, search consoles submitted, and analytics confirmed. The four CTW content stewards complete a single live training session on Keystatic, with a recorded video and a one-page reference card for follow-up. Performance monitoring stays active through ninety days of stabilization support. This is the point at which most agencies end their engagement. Ours doesn't.

Phase 5: Ongoing Partnership (Engine and Partnership Tiers)

For CTW selecting the Engine or Partnership modes, Phase 5 extends the engagement into the full ongoing program: the content production cadence documented in Section 6, the quarterly business reviews and competitive monitoring detailed in Section 13, and the proactive strategic input that keeps the engagement generating value past the first year. The senior team that ran the build runs the ongoing program. No handoffs.

The architecture this process produces is the subject of the next section.

Section 3

Recommended Site Architecture

The architecture below is built around three principles. Clarity for the visitor, who should reach any service, industry, or tool in one or two clicks. AEO depth across every page that matters, with structured FAQ blocks, schema markup, and plain-language content built for citation. And forward-rebrand readiness, so a future identity change propagates across the site without page-by-page rework.

The final sitemap selection, particularly the short list of named industries, happens jointly during the brand sprint with CTW's leadership. The structure here reflects our initial recommendation given what we know about the firm's current direction.

Notes on the Architecture

The current site lists twenty-eight industries. That breadth signals a generalist practice, which is the opposite of how CTW actually operates. We recommend consolidating to three to five named verticals, selected on the basis of the firm's recent advisory and capital event work. The candidates are construction and infrastructure, manufacturing and distribution, real estate, government contractors, and professional services firms. Final selection happens in the brand sprint with CTW's leadership holding the call.

Transaction Services and Capital Markets currently sit underneath Consulting on the existing site. We recommend elevating them as a peer to Audit, Tax, and Consulting in the top-level navigation. The advisory-led work that defines CTW's current direction belongs in the main navigation, not three clicks down.

The existing Newsroom contains posts from 2019 and 2020, most of which reference regulations that have since changed. We recommend retiring those posts and rebuilding the surface as Insights, with three subsections: Articles, Podcast, and Original Research. Final retain, rewrite, and retire decisions happen jointly during the audit phase. CTW preserves authority over what stays. Nothing gets archived without sign-off.

Tools is a new top-level section that gives each lead-generation surface its own page: the Capital Event Readiness Assessment, the R&D Tax Credit Estimator, and the Cost Segregation Estimator. Each tool gets a page because each one is an AEO retrieval target. Calculator-style queries carry real search volume, and a dedicated page gives each tool a real shot at direct citation.

Client-facing utility pages, Pay Invoice and the ShareFile portal link, live in the footer. Existing clients know where to find them. Keeping them out of the top navigation keeps the navigation focused on prospects.

Performance That Compounds

Most professional services websites built on WordPress today load in five to ten seconds on a mobile connection. CTW's current site sits squarely in that range. The cost of that lag isn't theoretical. Google's own research shows mobile visitors abandon a page at fifty-three percent likelihood when load time exceeds three seconds. The slower a site loads, the more prospects leave before they ever see the content the site was built to deliver.

CTW's new site will load in roughly one tenth of that time. We commit to a Largest Contentful Paint score under one second on every page at launch, measured on a standard 4G mobile connection. That's not a target we hit after months of tuning. That's the launch baseline.

The improvement comes from the architecture. Astro ships static HTML with near-zero JavaScript by default. Cloudflare delivers the static pages from edge servers physically close to every visitor. The entire stack favors performance over flexibility for its own sake.

Speed isn't only a user experience win. It's an SEO and AEO advantage. Google's ranking algorithms penalize slow pages. AI search engines increasingly favor sources that load and parse quickly. Faster sites get cited more often.

A CMS Built for Non-Developers

CTW's RFP makes ease of content updates a top requirement, with the expectation that four internal stewards will manage routine updates without needing developer involvement. Keystatic is built for exactly this case.

The interface is web-based and reads like a modern document editor: rich text, image uploads, drag-and-drop reordering, version history, simple preview before publish. Editing a page feels closer to editing a Google Doc than configuring a WordPress dashboard. The four CTW content stewards will be trained in a single live session, with a recorded video and a one-page reference guide for follow-up.

Routine updates (a new team member, a newsroom post, a service description revision, an upcoming event) take minutes. We expect CTW's team will handle the vast majority of content updates internally without calling us. When larger development support is needed (a new section, a major design change, a new lead generation tool), the codebase is structured so any competent developer can ship the change quickly. We are not building a black box. We are building a maintainable system.

Security and Reliability That Don't Require Maintenance

Most WordPress security incidents trace back to plugin vulnerabilities. The plugin ecosystem is enormous, the update cadence is constant, and the surface area for attack grows with every plugin a site adds. Professional services firms have been hit hard by this in recent years.

The stack we're proposing has dramatically less surface area. Static HTML pages can't be SQL-injected. There's no PHP runtime to exploit. Cloudflare provides DDoS protection, a web application firewall, and bot mitigation as part of the infrastructure. The site is protected by default. CTW isn't responsible for maintaining a security posture beyond keeping content current.

Uptime expectations: 99.99 percent or better, backed by Cloudflare's infrastructure SLA. In practical terms, the site doesn't go down.

Hosting Economics

WordPress managed hosting for a site of CTW's expected complexity typically runs fifteen hundred to six thousand dollars per year, depending on the hosting tier and traffic. The stack we're proposing runs between zero and three hundred dollars per year for hosting at expected traffic. The difference compounds across a five-year horizon.

We mention this not because hosting cost is a deciding factor, but because the savings are real and they free budget for content production and ongoing strategic work, which is where the real value is.

Forward-Rebrand Architecture

CTW's leadership flagged in the Q&A that a future full rebrand may happen and the site should be designed to accommodate it. We build for that by default.

Name references, logo files, color palettes, typography, and brand tokens live as configuration variables, not as values hardcoded into pages or components. When the rebrand happens, a designer updates the token file, the system rebuilds, and the new identity propagates across the entire site in under an hour. No page-by-page edits. No content rewrites required.

This isn't a feature we'll scope later. It's how the build is structured on day one.

CRM-Ready Data Layer

CTW will eventually select a CRM. The website will operate as if one is already in place. Forms, calculators, the Capital Event Readiness Assessment, and calendar bookings all write to a unified event schema designed to map cleanly to HubSpot or Salesforce.

When CTW signs the CRM engagement letter, the data layer is already structured to receive it. Migration takes days, not weeks. The lead generation work doesn't pause during the CRM rollout.

For most CPA firms of CTW's stage, we recommend HubSpot as the right entry point. The free CRM tier is genuinely useful, the paid tiers are an order of magnitude less expensive than Salesforce, the marketing automation is serviceable out of the box, and HubSpot's recently launched native AEO tracking tools integrate cleanly with the AEO foundation we'll build. Salesforce becomes the right call when the firm is ready to integrate deeply with audit workflow software or scale beyond fifty staff. Until then, HubSpot is the better tool for the job.

A Note on WordPress

WordPress has been the backbone of half the internet for two decades, and for many use cases it remains a solid choice. For CTW's specific requirements (performance, AEO discoverability, ease of content updates, security with minimal maintenance, forward-rebrand flexibility), it isn't the right tool. The plugin ecosystem creates ongoing fragility. The hosting overhead is unnecessary. The performance ceiling is lower. Most agencies will recommend WordPress because it's what they know how to build. We're recommending what we believe will serve CTW better.

Section 5

Lead Generation System

Most accounting firm websites are passive. Visitors arrive, browse, leave, and someone hopes they remember to follow up. CTW's site is built to work harder than that.

The lead generation architecture has five components. Each one captures a different kind of intent at a different stage of the buyer journey.

1. Calendar Booking with Intelligent Routing

Static contact forms turn high-intent moments into low-conversion experiences. A prospect who's ready to talk shouldn't have to wait for someone to read an email.

We'll integrate Calendly Teams across the site with intelligent routing. A short qualifying form (three questions: nature of inquiry, timeline, current client status) routes the prospect to the right partner or senior manager. Capital event inquiries reach the Capital Markets practice. Audit inquiries route round-robin among audit partners. Tax inquiries route to the right tax practice lead. Existing clients route to a different path entirely so we're not putting current relationships into the prospect funnel.

Calendly integrates natively with Microsoft 365 and Outlook, which is what CTW's team is already using. We'll configure availability windows, buffer rules, and fallback logic so a fully booked partner doesn't create a dead end. The setup is real work, not heroic.

2. Capital Event Readiness Assessment

This is the highest-leverage lead generation tool in the package. It replaces what most professional services firms still do (gated PDFs that anyone can download) with something materially better: a custom-written report generated for the prospect's actual business.

Here's how it works. A business owner on the site, somewhere in the two-to-five-year window before a sale or recapitalization, clicks "Get your readiness assessment." They answer eight to ten structured questions about their business: industry, revenue range, ownership structure, anticipated transaction type, timeline, current advisory situation, financial reporting maturity. Two open-ended fields capture context.

The submission triggers a workflow that calls Claude (or GPT-4) against a structured rubric we'll develop in the brand sprint with CTW's Capital Markets practice. The rubric defines what a good readiness story looks like, what the common gaps are at each business size and stage, and what the recommended next steps are based on the inputs. The AI generates a custom-written assessment in CTW's brand voice. Two to three pages. Specific to that prospect's situation.

The assessment lands in the prospect's inbox as a branded HTML email within minutes of submission. They get something written for them, not for a category. The submission and output are stored for sales follow-up, a Slack notification fires to the practice team, and a conversion event is logged for attribution.

Three things make this work that competitors won't replicate quickly. The output is delivered as a branded HTML email, not a downloadable file, so the perceived value sits with the firm that produced it. The custom-written content is materially more valuable than a generic guide, which raises the brand value of the firm behind it. And the architecture supports rapid expansion: once the first assessment is proven, we can spin up additional versions (audit readiness, tax planning health check, succession readiness) using the same plumbing.

3. Live Calculators

Two embedded calculators ship with the Foundation tier. Both are designed for AEO retrieval and lead capture.

The R&D Tax Credit Estimator takes industry, R&D wage base, supplies, and contract research inputs and produces a defensible estimate of the federal credit. Output captures the prospect's email at the "send me the detailed report" step.

The Cost Segregation Benefit Estimator takes property type, purchase price, building basis, and year placed in service. Output produces a benefit projection and recommended next steps.

Both calculators are SEO and AEO targets in their own right. The search volume on these keywords is real, and the conversion rate from calculator users is materially higher than average site traffic. Additional calculators (Section 199A QBI, Quality of Earnings preparation checklist, business valuation rough cut) are available as Phase 2 builds in the Engine and Partnership tiers.

4. AI Concierge (Engine and Partnership Tiers)

A conversational AI assistant trained on CTW's published content, service descriptions, and partner bios. Site visitors ask questions in natural language and get answers in CTW's voice within seconds. When intent is clear, the assistant offers calendar booking and routes to the appropriate practice.

The concierge runs on the same Cloudflare infrastructure as the Capital Event Readiness Assessment, with a vector store holding embeddings of CTW's content. Operating cost runs between fifty and two hundred dollars per month at expected traffic. The upside is a 24/7 prospect engagement layer that most CPA firms in the region won't have for at least two more years.

5. CRM-Ready Architecture

CTW doesn't have a CRM yet. The website is going to operate as if it does.

Every form, calculator, assessment, and calendar booking writes to a unified event schema. Server-side tracking captures form submissions, page views, and engagement signals. UTM parameter discipline is enforced on every external campaign link. Visitor profile data sits in a structured database, ready to be replayed into HubSpot, Salesforce, or whichever CRM CTW selects when the firm makes that decision.

In practical terms: the day CTW signs the engagement letter for a CRM, the data layer is already mapped to the contact properties spec. Migration is days, not weeks. The lead generation system doesn't pause while a CRM gets set up.

This is what we've spent more than a decade learning how to do.

The research is owned by the firm. It cites CTW as the source. It gets distributed to the trade press that covers the audiences CTW wants to reach. After four quarters the firm has a body of original work that doesn't exist anywhere else in the regional market and that AI search engines are already citing.

Trust, Compliance, and Accessibility

Accessibility

WCAG 2.2 AA conformance is built into the build from the start, not retrofitted. Semantic HTML across every page. Contrast ratios that pass at every type size. Full keyboard navigation. Visible focus states. Screen reader compatibility validated against current versions of NVDA and VoiceOver before launch. The site holds up to accessibility audit by any third party CTW chooses to engage.

We do not install an accessibility widget overlay. The overlay products sold by vendors in this space do not actually satisfy WCAG. They have been the basis of multiple recent ADA lawsuits against firms that believed they were covered. The site itself is accessible. A published accessibility statement page names the standard, lists the testing performed, and provides a direct contact path for any visitor who finds an issue.

Privacy and Data Handling

The existing CTW privacy policy is dated 2020 and predates the current California Privacy Rights Act requirements. The rebuild includes a modernized privacy policy and disclaimer set drafted against current CCPA standards. All form data, calculator inputs, assessment submissions, and calendar booking details travel encrypted with current TLS and are stored encrypted at rest.

A cookie consent surface appears for visitors in regulatory environments that require explicit consent. Analytics run server-side where possible to reduce reliance on third-party tracking scripts. All visitor data lives on infrastructure CTW controls, with retention policies documented on the privacy page itself.

Security Posture

Cloudflare provides the infrastructure security baseline: DDoS protection, web application firewall, bot mitigation, automatic SSL, and edge-level rate limiting. The static site architecture removes the most common attack vectors on professional services firms. There is no SQL database to inject against. There is no PHP runtime to exploit. There are no plugins requiring constant security patching.

The site's security posture is published as a trust signal alongside the privacy and accessibility statements. Middle-market buyers and CFOs evaluating professional advisors read these pages. A modern security posture, stated plainly, is a differentiator in a category where most firms say nothing.
